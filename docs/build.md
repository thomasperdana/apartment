Building a 100-unit apartment complex means clearing four gates in order: the land must be usable, the project must pencil out financially, the city must approve it, and then it must be built and leased. The details vary by country, state, and city. This is a general overview, with US terms.

## 1. Land and site control
- **Site that fits the use:** Zoning must allow multifamily at your density, or you need a rezoning or variance. Check height, setbacks, parking ratio, lot coverage, and floor-area ratio.
- **Site due diligence:** Survey, title search, environmental Phase I (and Phase II if flagged), soil and geotechnical tests, utility capacity (water, sewer, power), flood zone status, and traffic impact.
- **Control of the land:** An option or purchase contract, usually with contingency periods so you can walk away if approvals or tests fail.

## 2. Feasibility and financing plan
- **Pro forma:** Estimate rents by unit type and market, vacancy, operating expenses, and a target yield on cost (often 5.5–7% today, but check your market). Use the pro forma to work backward to the maximum land price and build cost you can afford.
- **Capital stack:** Typically a construction loan (about 55–70% of cost), sponsor equity (often 25–40%), and sometimes mezzanine debt or preferred equity. Lenders want to see experienced sponsors, a guarantor with liquidity, and a fully budgeted plan.
- **Permanent financing:** A take-out plan for after construction, such as Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, HUD, or a bank loan.
- **Budget:** Hard costs (construction, often the largest share), soft costs (design, permits, legal, insurance, interest), a 5–10% contingency, and lease-up reserves. Per-unit costs vary widely by market, from well under $200k to over $400k per unit.

## 3. Team
- Developer or sponsor, with a track record
- Architect and civil, structural, MEP, and landscape engineers
- Land-use attorney and zoning consultant
- General contractor (often hired via a GMP contract or design-build)
- Lender, equity partners, and a property manager
- Accountant and insurance broker

## 4. Entitlements and permits
- **Pre-application meeting** with the planning department
- **Site plan and zoning approval**, sometimes with public hearings, neighbor notice, and design review
- **Environmental review** if required, such as CEQA in California or NEPA for federal funding
- **Building permits:** Structural, plumbing, electrical, mechanical, fire, and grading, plus utility connection permits
- **Code compliance:** The local building code (usually based on the IBC), fire code and fire access, accessibility (ADA/FHA: about 5% of units fully accessible and more adaptable units, plus accessible routes), energy code, and stormwater management
- **Affordable housing requirements,** if the city or a funding source requires a share of units at below-market rents, or if you're using tax credits or bonds

Permitting often takes 6–18 months or more, depending on the city and whether a rezoning is needed.

## 5. Construction
- **Procurement:** Bid packages or GMP contract, subcontractor selection, and long-lead items like elevators, windows, and switchgear.
- **Insurance and bonds:** Builder's risk, general liability, and payment and performance bonds.
- **Inspections:** Required at each stage by the building department, plus lender inspections to release construction draws.
- **Schedule:** Typically 18–30 months for a 100-unit wood-frame or podium project. Podium projects with concrete and steel take longer.
- **Certificate of occupancy** once inspections pass.

## 6. Lease-up and operations
- Marketing, leasing staff, and a leasing office or model unit
- Property management systems, resident screening, and lease documents that comply with local tenant law
- Lease-up typically takes 6–18 months to stabilize at 90–95% occupancy, which the lender will want before converting the construction loan to permanent financing
- Ongoing budgets for maintenance, reserves, insurance, property taxes, and utilities

## Rough timeline
Land acquisition to stabilized occupancy usually runs **3–5 years**, with the largest variables being entitlements and financing.
